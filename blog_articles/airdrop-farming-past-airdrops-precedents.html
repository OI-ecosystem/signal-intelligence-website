Airdrop Farming Is Back in Fashion. What Happened to the Big Airdrops Before?

11 Nov 2026 5 min read Signal Intelligence Research

Past airdrops have more often been followed by falling prices than rising ones: Uniswap's token rose in its first week in 2020, but Arbitrum and Wormhole traded well below their early highs within days, and a Keyrock study found 88% of tokens launched alongside airdrops in 2024 declined. Vendor research on this year's airdrops points the same way, with 35 of 46 tokens studied trading below their first-week median price.

Airdrop farming, using new crypto apps and test networks in the hope of being rewarded with free tokens when a project launches, is once again one of the most common topics in crypto conversation this autumn. The question many farmers are asking is simple: how often has it actually paid, and what usually happens to an airdropped token after it starts trading?

The most detailed recent look at that question comes from Bitquery, a blockchain data company, which published a study of 51 large airdrops from 2026 on 30 September, using data to 29 September. It is vendor research rather than independent reporting, but its method and data are published. Of the 46 tokens that had been trading for at least 30 days, 35 were below their first-week median price, with a median change of minus 48%. Claimer behaviour was just as telling: in the median airdrop, 73% of claimers moved their tokens within a day, 23% still held at least half after 30 days, and 8% after 90 days. The rewards were also concentrated, with the top 10% of claimers taking 73% of tokens in the median drop.

Those figures sound stark, but they are not new. The history of large airdrops gives useful reference points.

2020: Uniswap, the airdrop that set the template

In September 2020, Uniswap Labs made 400 UNI claimable by each address that had ever used the Uniswap v1 or v2 contracts, spread across 251,534 historical user addresses. In total that was 100,613,600 UNI, about 10.06% of the 1 billion tokens minted. The Block reported that around 141,000 addresses had claimed within four days.

Uniswap is the case farmers remember, and for good reason. According to CoinDesk, UNI traded at $2.97 on its first day and had edged past $4.80 about a week later, by which point just under $560 million worth had been claimed by about 190,000 addresses. The reward went to people who had used the product before anyone knew a token was coming, which is the key point: there was, in effect, nothing to farm in advance.

2023: Arbitrum, and the chaos of a crowded launch

By the time Arbitrum distributed ARB on 23 March 2023, airdrops were expected, and many users had positioned for one. The Arbitrum Foundation allocated 1.162 billion ARB, 11.62% of the initial supply, to users, plus 113 million ARB to DAOs. CoinDesk reported that 625,143 wallets received the airdrop.

The first day showed how unreliable a single launch price can be. CoinDesk reported ARB trading at $3.99 on Uniswap, with prices on some exchanges briefly as high as $14 on Bybit, before the token settled at $1.38 later that day. Decrypt reported the next day that ARB was at $1.27 and that 81% of the airdropped tokens had already been claimed. Prices varied so widely across venues in the first hours that the honest description is a range, not a number.

2024: the airdrop wave and the sybil crackdown

2024 brought a run of very large airdrops. Wormhole distributed 678,823,000 W, 6.78% of its 10 billion supply, to nearly 400,000 wallets on 3 April 2024. The Block reported that the token went from a high of $1.66 to a low of $1.25, a fall of nearly 25% within hours, before recovering to $1.50. A week later, Decrypt reported W at $0.82, down 50.6% from its debut price.

Across the year the pattern was broad. A study by the market maker Keyrock, reported by DL News in September 2024, found that 88% of tokens launched alongside airdrops that year had declined in price, with most falling within 15 days, and that after three months few showed a positive result.

2024 was also the year projects pushed back hardest against farmers. LayerZero ran a self-reporting window in which, according to its chief executive, up to 100,000 addresses identified themselves as sybils, multiple wallets controlled by one person. The Block later reported that more than 800,000 addresses were flagged as potential sybils, and that 1.28 million of the 6 million unique wallets in the snapshot were eligible. Bitquery's 2026 data shows the issue has not gone away: in the median drop it studied, 11% of claimers sat in linked wallet groups.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Selling pressure at launch. In every case above, a large share of tokens was claimed within days, and Bitquery's 2026 figures on how quickly claimers move tokens fit the earlier pattern of early declines.
  • Who gets paid. Uniswap rewarded past users who had not been farming. Later airdrops were expected in advance, attracted large numbers of farmers, and increasingly filtered wallets out.
  • Concentration. The 2026 data suggests most of the value goes to a small share of claimers, which matters for anyone estimating what a typical wallet receives.
  • Measurement. Results depend heavily on the reference price. A token can look up or down sharply depending on whether it is measured from the first trade, the first-day close or the first-week median.

What to watch

For anyone following the current wave of farming, the useful facts are concrete and checkable. What share of supply goes to the community, and how much of it is unlocked at launch? Does the project publish its eligibility and sybil rules before the snapshot? And how much of the token trades on its first day compared with the amount still locked for teams and investors?

The record of past airdrops is mixed, with one celebrated early success and many tokens that traded lower within weeks. Past airdrops do not tell us how any particular launch will perform. Signal Intelligence will keep tracking the facts as they develop.
