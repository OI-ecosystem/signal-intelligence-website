Silver Is More Than 60% Below Its January Record. Here Is What Followed the 1980 and 2011 Peaks.

21 Nov 2026 4 min read Signal Intelligence Research

Silver has fallen this far from a record twice before: after it peaked near $50 in January 1980 and in April 2011, it fell to $10.80 within ten weeks in 1980 and to $13.695 by December 2015, and neither peak was surpassed until October 2025. Silver was trading near $41.86 on 20 November, more than 60% below its late-January record, and this time the stated driver is expected US rate rises rather than an exchange crackdown, so the precedents describe past paths rather than a forecast.

On 20 November silver was trading near $41.86 an ounce, down 2.4% on the day, according to USAGOLD's daily market report. A week earlier, on 13 November, Kitco reported spot silver at $48.58, already down 6% on that week. On 12 November the price fell back below $50 after a hotter-than-expected US inflation report lifted the dollar to a 19-month high, and on 16 November Forbes reported that silver had touched its lowest price since October 2025.

The fall from the peak is large. Silver set a record of more than $121 an ounce in late January, and by 16 November it was trading roughly 61% below that level, USAGOLD reported. Forbes Advisor put silver down 36.94% since the start of the year as of 19 November. Trading Economics described an early reversal in which silver tumbled to roughly $64 in February as leveraged positions were unwound.

The reasons given this week are about interest rates rather than supply. Saxo Bank told Forbes that persistent inflation continued to fuel expectations of further US rate hikes, and by 20 November CME's FedWatch tool priced a 99.3% chance of a quarter-point rise in December. Meanwhile the Silver Institute, in research by Metals Focus reported by Kitco on 13 November, still expects a sixth consecutive market deficit in 2026, of 35 million ounces.

So the question many readers are asking is a fair one: has silver fallen this far from a record before, and what came next? It has, twice in the past five decades, and neither recovery was quick.

1980: the Hunt brothers and Silver Thursday

The first precedent is the most famous. Between January and December 1979, the ABA Journal recounts, the price of silver rose from $6 to $32 an ounce as the Hunt brothers built up a huge position. It peaked at $49.45 in mid-January 1980, according to Britannica and the ABA Journal.

The rules then changed. On 21 January 1980, at the urging of regulators, the Commodity Exchange enacted rules on silver purchases that in effect required the Hunts to sell, the ABA Journal reports. On Thursday 27 March 1980, the day that became known as Silver Thursday, silver fell to $10.80, which Britannica describes as the metal's biggest single collapse. New federal regulation of commodity markets followed, especially for margin trading.

The decline did not stop there. MetalCharts' price history shows silver trading as low as $4.88 in June 1982. On the same data, silver did not clear $50 an ounce again until October 2025.

2011: margin increases and the worst week since 1975

The second precedent came three decades later. Silver futures reached a three-decade high of $49.845 on 25 April 2011, Bloomberg News reported. CME Group then raised margin requirements for new speculative positions by 84% in less than two weeks, with five increases announced from 25 April.

The selling was fast. In the week to 6 May 2011, silver futures fell 27.4%, closing at $35.287, which Bloomberg called the worst week since at least 1975, CoinNews reported. Even after that week, silver was still 14.1% higher for the year.

The longer decline came later. On 14 December 2015, Comex silver settled at $13.695, its lowest close since July 2009, CoinNews reported. The GFMS World Silver Survey put the average price for 2015 at $15.68. As in 1980, the old peak stood until October 2025.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Rules and rates. In 1980 the turn followed exchange rule changes aimed at a concentrated position, and in 2011 it followed a run of margin increases. This year the stated driver is expectations of higher US interest rates and a strong dollar, a broader force than an exchange rule.
  • Scale of the run-up. Both earlier peaks came close to $50. This year's record was more than twice that, after silver first cleared $50 in October 2025, so the absolute moves are larger.
  • Supply and demand. The Silver Institute's research still points to a deficit in 2026, but also to weaker demand: total industrial demand is forecast to fall 3% to 640 million ounces, and bar and coin demand to fall 2% to 215 million ounces. Global silver exchange-traded product holdings stood at 1.25 billion ounces on 3 November, down 4% since the start of the year.
  • Measures. The precedents mix futures settlements, spot quotes and data-site histories, and record figures differ slightly by source. They show the shape of past declines, not a like-for-like comparison with 2026.

What to watch

The useful questions are factual ones. Does the Federal Reserve raise rates in December, as futures markets now expect? Do silver exchange-traded product holdings keep falling, or do they stabilise? And when the Silver Institute publishes its next World Silver Survey, does the 2026 deficit come in near the 35 million ounces now forecast?

The precedents do not tell us how this cycle will end. They show that silver has fallen far from a record before, and that after both the 1980 and 2011 peaks the earlier high was not regained for many years. The conditions this time are different, and they could not be seen in advance then either. Signal Intelligence will keep tracking the facts as they come in.
