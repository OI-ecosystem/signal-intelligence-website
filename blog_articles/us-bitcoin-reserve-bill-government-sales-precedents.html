A US Bill Would Lock Away Federal Bitcoin for 20 Years. Here Is What Followed Earlier Government Sales.

27 Nov 2026 5 min read Signal Intelligence Research

A House committee has sent the full House a bill that would bar the US government from selling its bitcoin for at least 20 years, though The Block judges its chances of passing this Congress to be low. Earlier government sales of scarce assets, from Britain’s gold in 1999 to the US Silk Road auction in 2014 and Germany’s bitcoin in 2024, were each followed by rises that made the timing look costly in hindsight.

A bill that would stop the US government selling its bitcoin for at least 20 years cleared a House committee this week, in the same month that bitcoin has fallen about 22% and US spot bitcoin funds have recorded their largest monthly outflow. It raises an old question in a new market: what has happened when governments sold large reserves of a scarce asset, and what did the timing cost them?

What is happening now

The Block reported that the House Financial Services Committee has advanced H.R. 8957, the American Reserve Modernization Act of 2026, introduced by Rep. Nick Begich on 21 May. The committee approved it 28-21 on a party-line vote on 16 September and formally sent the revised bill to the full House on 24 November, where it sits on the Union Calendar as Calendar No. 789.

The bill would put into law a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve. Within 180 days of enactment, the Treasury secretary could set up the reserve and a separate Digital Asset Stockpile for other cryptocurrencies. Bitcoin deposited in the reserve could not be "sold, swapped, auctioned, encumbered, or otherwise disposed of" for at least 20 years. The bill also includes proof-of-reserve reporting and asks federal agencies to report what they already hold.

It builds on the executive order of March 2025, which set up a reserve funded mainly with forfeited bitcoin and stated that the United States would not sell bitcoin deposited into it. The White House fact sheet at the time claimed that earlier sales of seized bitcoin had already cost taxpayers more than $17 billion. An executive order can be changed by a later president; a law would need Congress to undo it.

The path to passage looks narrow. The Block noted that the current Congress is expected to adjourn within weeks and that Democrats are set to control both chambers from January, so a bill that does not pass now would have to be reintroduced. On Polymarket, the implied chance of the US establishing a national bitcoin reserve by the end of this year was 4%, down from 60% about a year ago.

The market backdrop is weak. CoinDesk, citing SoSoValue data, reported that US spot bitcoin ETFs lost a net $5.39 billion in November up to 25 November, the largest monthly outflow since they launched in January 2024, and that bitcoin had fallen 22% this month to around $60,000.

2014: the Silk Road auction

The US government has sold seized bitcoin before. In June 2014 the US Marshals Service auctioned 29,656 bitcoin seized in the Silk Road case. PBS reported that the coins, which the government valued at $17.4 million, were sold over a 12-hour period, and that about 175,000 of the site's bitcoin remained in government hands. Bitcoin was then trading at $595.15, according to CoinDesk figures cited by PBS, after falling 7% on news of the auction.

Fortune reported that the auction drew 63 offers from 45 registered bidders, and that venture capitalist Tim Draper won the full lot. The price he paid was not disclosed. Those coins were sold for a few hundred dollars each; bitcoin is now trading at roughly a hundred times that level.

2024: Germany sells almost 50,000 bitcoin

A larger and more recent case came from Germany. Authorities in Saxony said they sold about 49,858 bitcoin, seized in the Movie2k film-piracy case, between 19 June and 12 July 2024, and netted about €2.6 billion, The Block reported. The case itself was not concluded, and the authorities cited the risk of a loss in value of around 10% or more as a reason to sell early.

Cointelegraph reported an average sale price of about $57,900. The last transfer, of 3,846.05 BTC, went out on 12 July. Bitcoin climbed back above $60,000 on 14 July, and by May 2025, with bitcoin above $104,700, blockchain analytics firm Arkham estimated that the coins would have been worth $5.24 billion if they had been kept.

1999: Britain's gold sales

The best-known precedent is not in crypto at all. On 7 May 1999 the UK Treasury announced that it would sell about 415 tonnes of the country's 715 tonnes of gold reserves, the National Audit Office recorded. According to the London Bullion Market Association's journal, the decision and its advance announcement knocked prices about 10% lower before the first auction on 6 July, and gold went on to set 18 fresh 20-year lows inside three months.

Bullion by Post put the average sale price over the 1999 to 2002 programme at $275 an ounce, for a total of about $3.5 billion. The timing became notorious. In September 1999 gold had its sharpest one-day rise since 1982, the start of a two-week gain of 25%, and by the peaks of 2011 to 2013 it had risen well over five-fold in dollars, the LBMA journal noted. The LBMA journal titled its 20-year retrospective "Gordon's Brown Bottom", after the then Chancellor, Gordon Brown.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Announced selling moves prices. In 1999 the advance notice of UK sales weighed on gold before a single bar was sold, and in 2014 bitcoin fell 7% on news of the Marshals' auction. A legal lock-up would remove that kind of known government supply for as long as it lasts.
  • Sellers had reasons. Britain's sales were a deliberate reserve policy, and Saxony was holding coins in a criminal case that had not ended and cited the risk of a fall in value. Hindsight makes those sales look badly timed; at the time they were defensible choices.
  • Law versus policy. The UK gold sales and the German bitcoin sales were decisions by governments acting within their powers. H.R. 8957 would make a lock-up a legal requirement, which is a stronger commitment than an executive order, and only if it passes.
  • A weaker market. This debate is happening while bitcoin is falling and ETF money is leaving. Earlier sales took place in very different conditions, and none of them tells us what bitcoin does next.

What to watch

The checkable markers are whether H.R. 8957 reaches a House floor vote before Congress adjourns, whether it is reintroduced in the new Congress, any change to the March 2025 executive order, agency reports on existing federal holdings, and the monthly spot ETF flow figures.

Governments that sold scarce assets at a low have been criticised for it ever since, but those precedents describe what happened afterwards, not what will happen this time. Signal Intelligence will keep tracking the facts as they develop.
