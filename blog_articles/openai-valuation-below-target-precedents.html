OpenAI's New Round Came In Below Its First Target. Tech Has Been Here Before.

8 Nov 2026 4 min read Signal Intelligence Research

Yes: Facebook in 2012, Uber in 2019 and WeWork in 2019 all priced below what had been expected, and what followed ranged from a slow recovery to bankruptcy. OpenAI's reported $11 billion raise at a $1.25 trillion valuation, below the $1.4 trillion target reported in September, is a private round rather than a listing, which changes how far those precedents carry.

According to Bloomberg, OpenAI has raised $11 billion so far in a new funding round that values the company at $1.25 trillion. The figures were reported on 6 November 2026, citing people familiar with the matter, and OpenAI declined to comment. The $11 billion is the first part of a round that has not yet closed; one person told Bloomberg it could reach about $20 billion over the coming weeks.

What has drawn attention is the gap to earlier reports. On 29 September, Bloomberg reported that OpenAI was aiming to raise $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation. The figures reported this week sit below both numbers. OpenAI's chief financial officer, Sarah Friar, has described the new price as almost 50% above March, when the company raised $122 billion at an $852 billion valuation, and has disputed reports of an even higher initial ask. TechCrunch, citing Reuters, notes that OpenAI is expected to go public next year.

So the natural question is whether a leading technology company has priced itself below its first expectations before, and what happened afterwards. It has, several times. Three cases from the last fifteen years show how wide the range of outcomes can be.

Facebook, 2012: a full price, then a long wait

Facebook priced its initial public offering at $38 a share on 17 May 2012, valuing the company at $104 billion and raising $16 billion for the company and its early investors. Trading began on Nasdaq the next day. The shares closed the first day at $38.23, only just above the offer price, and by the next trading day had closed at about $34.03, down 11%.

The fall continued through the summer. On 4 September 2012 the shares touched an intraday low of $17.55. Facebook did not close above its IPO price again until 2 August 2013, at $38.05, more than fourteen months after listing. In this case the reset came after the listing, through trading, and lasted more than a year.

Uber, 2019: the reset happened before the listing

In October 2018, the Wall Street Journal reported that proposals from Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley had valued Uber at up to $120 billion in a possible IPO. When Uber priced its shares on 9 May 2019, it set them at $45, the bottom of its $44 to $50 range. That gave a fully diluted valuation of about $82.4 billion, according to CNBC, and the offering raised about $8.1 billion.

The lower price did not stop the shares falling at first. Uber opened on the New York Stock Exchange on 10 May 2019 at $42 and closed the day at $41.57, more than 7% below the offer price. Here the company had already accepted a valuation well short of the one discussed months earlier, and the first day still went against it.

WeWork, 2019: when the reset kept going

WeWork is the cautionary case. In January 2019, SoftBank invested a further $2 billion in a deal that WeWork said valued it at $47 billion. By September, as WeWork prepared to list, Reuters reported that it might seek an IPO valuation of between $10 billion and $12 billion. On 24 September 2019 the company confirmed that its co-founder, Adam Neumann, would step down as chief executive, and on 30 September it said it would withdraw its IPO.

In October 2019 SoftBank took control of the company in a rescue reported to value WeWork at about $8 billion. WeWork eventually reached the stock market through a merger with BowX, a special purpose acquisition company, and began trading on the NYSE on 21 October 2021. On 6 November 2023 it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Private versus public. All three precedents were about public listings, where the market set a daily price. OpenAI's round is a private funding round, negotiated with a smaller group of investors, so there is no daily price to watch.
  • Direction of the step. Uber and WeWork priced below their own earlier private or proposed valuations. OpenAI's reported $1.25 trillion is below the September target, but on its CFO's account it is still well above the $852 billion valuation of March.
  • Size of the gap. WeWork's valuation talk fell from $47 billion to between $10 billion and $12 billion within a year. The gap between OpenAI's reported target and its reported price is much narrower.
  • What came next varied widely. Facebook spent more than a year below its IPO price before passing it; WeWork withdrew its listing and later went bankrupt. The precedents agree only that a lower-than-hoped price was the start of the story, not the end.

What to watch

The useful questions now are factual ones. Does the round close, and at what final size, compared with the roughly $20 billion that has been reported? Who are the named investors once terms are disclosed? And what does OpenAI say about the timing of a public listing, which would put a market price on the company for the first time?

Past valuation resets do not tell us how this one will play out, and each of the three cases above ended differently. They do show that the gap between an early target and a final price is worth reading carefully, alongside the facts that follow it.
