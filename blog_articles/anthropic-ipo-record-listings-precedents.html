Anthropic’s IPO Could Be the Biggest Ever. Here Is What Followed Earlier Record Listings.

16 Nov 2026 5 min read Signal Intelligence Research

Record listings have started strongly before and then struggled: Alibaba in 2014 and Saudi Aramco in 2019 both rose sharply on debut, then fell below their offer prices within a year, while SpaceX, the record holder since June 2026, ended its first week 37% above its IPO price. Anthropic’s offering, which with $26 billion of share commitments would come to about $90 billion, has not yet priced, so these precedents show a range of outcomes rather than a forecast.

Anthropic, the developer of the Claude AI models, set out the terms of its initial public offering in a filing on 10 November 2026. According to Silicon Republic, the shares were priced in a range of $70 to $78, valuing the company at between $1.26 trillion and $1.41 trillion, short of earlier expectations of up to $2 trillion. The company plans to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT later this month.

On 12 November Bloomberg reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that the offering was about six times oversubscribed. Anthropic is seeking to raise around $64 billion in the IPO itself, alongside $26 billion of stock purchase commitments from Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC. Taken together, that would surpass the amount SpaceX raised in its debut in June. The Decoder reports that Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are managing the listing. Silicon Republic notes that details, including demand for the shares, could still change.

So the question readers are asking is a natural one: when a listing has broken the record for size before, what happened to the shares afterwards? Three earlier record holders give a useful range.

Alibaba, 2014: a big first day, then a fall below the offer price

Alibaba priced its New York listing at $68 a share. After the banks released additional “green shoe” shares, the offering was worth $25 billion, up from an original $21.8 billion, according to Fortune. That surpassed the $24.3 billion raised by Agricultural Bank of China and made it the biggest IPO ever at the time.

The debut on 19 September 2014 was strong. The shares opened more than 36% above the offer price, at $92.70, and closed the first day at $93.89. The early gains did not last. On Monday 24 August 2015, during a global market rout in which the Shanghai Composite fell 8.5%, Alibaba’s shares fell 3.5% to close at $65.8, below their $68 issue price for the first time, according to China Daily. Within a year, the record listing had gone from a 36% first-day gain to trading below what IPO buyers had paid.

Saudi Aramco, 2019: the target hit, then an oil shock

Saudi Aramco priced its IPO at 32 riyals ($8.53) a share, the top of its indicative range, raising $25.6 billion and beating Alibaba’s record, Al Jazeera reported on 5 December 2019. The deal valued the company at $1.7 trillion. In January 2020 Aramco exercised its green shoe option to sell 450 million more shares, taking the offering to a record $29.4 billion.

The shares began trading in Riyadh on 11 December 2019. According to Bloomberg, an initial rally of about 20% in the first two days lifted the valuation to the $2 trillion that Saudi Arabia’s leadership had been seeking. Less than three months later, the picture had changed. On Sunday 8 March 2020, after OPEC+ talks broke down and oil prices fell around 10%, Aramco’s shares dropped below the IPO price for the first time, closing down 9.1% at 30 riyals. Bloomberg calculated that more than $400 billion of market value had been wiped out in about three months.

SpaceX, 2026: the current record holder

The record Anthropic is measured against is only five months old. On 11 June 2026, SpaceX sold 555,555,555 shares at $135 each, raising $75 billion, according to NPR. That was the biggest IPO on record, well above Aramco’s $29.4 billion, and the offer price valued SpaceX at around $1.75 trillion. The shares, under the ticker SPCX, closed 19% higher on their first day of trading, 12 June, Bloomberg reported, and ended the first week 37% above the $135 price, with a market value of $2.4 trillion.

Five months is a short history. The Alibaba and Aramco cases show that a strong first week does not settle how a record listing fares over the following year, and SpaceX’s story as a public company is still being written.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Strong demand before the debut is common. Aramco priced at the top of its range, and Alibaba and SpaceX both rose sharply on their first day. Reported oversubscription for Anthropic fits that pattern, but in none of the earlier cases did early demand decide what came later.
  • The setbacks came from outside. Alibaba first slipped below its offer price during a global sell-off led by Chinese shares; Aramco did so when an oil price war hit its core market. In both cases the trigger was a shock to the wider market or industry rather than news about the company alone.
  • The valuation has already moved. Anthropic’s range of $1.26 trillion to $1.41 trillion is below earlier talk of up to $2 trillion, Silicon Republic reports, though above the $965 billion of its last private round. Aramco, by contrast, reached its $2 trillion target only through trading after the listing.
  • The structure is different. About $26 billion of Anthropic’s total comes from commitments by technology companies, including chip makers and cloud providers, rather than from the public offering. None of the three precedents had a strategic component on that scale.

What to watch

The useful questions now are factual ones. Where within, or outside, the $70 to $78 range does the final price land? How much does the offering finally raise, and does the total, including the commitments, end up above SpaceX’s $75 billion? And how do AI shares more broadly trade into the listing, given the swings of recent weeks?

Earlier record listings do not tell us how this one will go, and the three cases above followed different paths after strong starts. They do show that the first days of trading are only the start of the record, and that wider market shocks have mattered as much as the company itself.
