New York Art Auctions Are Aiming for $2bn Again. Here Is How the Last Two Slumps Ended.

17 Nov 2026 5 min read Signal Intelligence Research

The art market has recovered from two deep slumps in living memory: after 1990 sales stayed far below their peak for years, while after the 2008 crisis a new auction record for a painting came in May 2010. New York's November sales are now forecast at $2.04bn or more after auction sales fell 25% in 2024, and the speed of this year's rebound is what sets it apart.

Sotheby's opened New York's November auction week on 16 November with back-to-back evening sales that totalled $378.7m with fees, The Art Newspaper reported. The single-owner Baum collection sold all 24 of its lots for $109.9m with fees, against an estimate of $74.4m to $109m, although about a third of the lots hammered below their low estimates. The Now and Contemporary evening sale that followed made $268.7m with fees, which Sotheby's described as its highest total for that sale since May 2024. Records were set for Yayoi Kusama, at $15m with fees, and for Sam Francis, at $17.1m.

The week is expected to be large. Before the sales, The Art Newspaper reported that Sotheby's, Christie's, Phillips and Bonhams expected to bring in at least $2.04bn in New York this month, and possibly more than $2.4bn. Sotheby's alone projected more than $1.1bn, which it called a house record for a marquee season, with about 40% of that from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection of 12 Impressionist, Post-Impressionist and Fauvist paintings, offered on 18 November. Christie's expected between $823m and $1.1bn.

That follows a strong first half. Christie's public auction sales rose 71% to $3.5bn in the first six months of 2026, and Sotheby's rose 59% to $3.4bn, according to The Art Newspaper's report on their half-year results.

The rebound starts from a low base. The Art Basel and UBS Global Art Market Report 2025 estimated that global art sales fell 12% to $57.5bn in 2024, the second year of decline, and that public auction sales fell 25% to $19.0bn. Sales of fine art at auction above $10m fell 45% in 2024, after a 40% fall in 2023. So the question collectors and dealers are asking is a fair one: has the art market climbed out of a slump like this before, and how did the recovery go? It has, twice in recent decades, and the two recoveries looked very different.

1990: a record, then a long winter

The first precedent is the end of the late-1980s boom. On 15 May 1990 Vincent van Gogh's Portrait of Dr Gachet sold at Christie's New York for $82.5m, bought by the Japanese businessman Ryoei Saito. The Art Newspaper later wrote that the market came to an abrupt halt after June 1990, when Japanese buyers withdrew, with the Gulf War adding to the uncertainty.

The November 1990 sales showed how quickly sentiment had changed. At Sotheby's New York on 6 November, some 56% of the works offered failed to sell, Time magazine reported, and at Christie's the next night 48% went unsold. Time noted that Sotheby's shares, at about $35 in October 1989, had fallen to about $10.

The slump lasted for years. In May 1993 Sotheby's raised $8.1m in its New York contemporary sales, against $89.4m at the November 1989 peak, according to The Art Newspaper. The recovery came gradually: the November Impressionist and Modern Part I sales in New York made $131.9m in 1993 and $220m in 1994, and by November 1999 Impressionist and Modern auctions in New York totalled $402.8m.

2008: a sharp fall, then a fast return

The second precedent is the financial crisis. The Fiscal Times reported that the art market came crashing down to earth in the summer of 2008, and that Christie's sales fell 35% to $3.3bn in 2009.

The recovery at the top of the market came quickly. On 4 May 2010 Pablo Picasso's Nude, Green Leaves and Bust sold at Christie's New York for $106.5m, which NPR reported as the most expensive painting ever sold at auction, ahead of the $104.3m paid for Alberto Giacometti's sculpture Walking Man I, the previous auction record. The Fiscal Times asked in its headline: what recession?

What is similar, and what is different

  • Speed. After 1990 the decline ran for years, and in 1993 sales were still far below the peak. After 2008 records returned within two years. This time, Christie's and Sotheby's reported large rises in the first half of 2026, after two years of decline in global sales.
  • Who is selling. Much of this season rests on single-owner collections. On 16 November, single-owner works made $239m with fees, almost 60% of the evening total, The Art Newspaper reported. Large estates tend to bring material to market regardless of the cycle, so strong totals from them do not by themselves show broad demand.
  • Depth of bidding. In 1990 the warning signs were unsold rates of around half. This week, the Baum sale sold every lot, but a third of its lots hammered below their low estimates, and a Franz Kline sold 55% below its low estimate of $4m. Five lots went unsold in the contemporary sale.
  • Measures. The precedents mix different figures: individual sale totals, one company's annual sales, and single record prices. They show the shape of past recoveries, not a like-for-like comparison with 2026.

What to watch

The useful questions are factual ones. Do the remaining sales this week, including Christie's 20th-century sale on 17 November and the Blaquier-Arrieta collection on 18 November, meet their estimates? Does the week's total reach the $2.04bn to $2.4bn the houses expected? And when the Art Basel and UBS report for 2025 and full-year results for 2026 are published, do they confirm that auction sales have turned?

The precedents do not tell us how this cycle will end. They show that the art market has recovered from slumps before, once slowly over several years and once within two years, and that the pace depended on who was buying and on conditions that could not be seen at the low.
