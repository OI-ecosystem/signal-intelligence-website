Nvidia Has Lost Its Founder as CEO. Here Is What Followed Earlier Tech Leadership Changes.

20 Nov 2026 5 min read Signal Intelligence Research

Sudden changes at the top of large technology companies have been followed by very different paths: Apple's shares fell about 5% after hours when Steve Jobs resigned as chief executive in 2011, and the company later became the first listed company worth $1 trillion, while Intel's shares jumped 15% when it named a permanent chief executive a little over three months after Pat Gelsinger's exit. Nvidia, whose co-founder Jensen Huang resigned on 12 November 2026 after 33 years, is at the start of that process, with an interim chief executive and a search under way, so these cases show a range of outcomes rather than a forecast.

On Thursday 12 November 2026, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and a member of the board, ending a 33-year run at the company he co-founded, according to The San Francisco Standard. The Next Web reported that the resignation was effective immediately. The company did not give a reason for his departure, China Daily reported.

The chief financial officer, Colette Kress, took over as interim chief executive and keeps her other roles. She joined Nvidia in September 2013 after nearly 25 years in finance at companies including Cisco and Microsoft, the SF Standard reported. The board named Harvey C. Jones as its first chair and is searching for a permanent successor, considering both internal and external candidates, Reuters reported.

The shares had closed on Wednesday 11 November at $173.73 and opened on Thursday at $151.00, according to the SF Standard. Reuters later reported the shares down about 4.8%, paring earlier losses. The Wealth Advisor noted that the stock had lost nearly 30% over the previous month.

Five days later, on 17 November, Nvidia reported its first results without Huang. Revenue for the quarter to 25 October was $115.8 billion, up 103% on a year earlier and above the $109.1 billion analysts had expected, Fox Business reported. Data-centre revenue was $107.6 billion, up 110%, according to w.media. Nvidia guided to revenue of about $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, for the current quarter, which includes an extra week. Kress presented the results, and Fox Business reported the shares up more than 3.4% after hours.

So the question readers are asking is a natural one: when a large technology company has changed its chief executive suddenly before, what happened next? Two cases since 2011 give a useful range.

Apple, 2011: a founder steps back, the chosen successor takes over

On Wednesday 24 August 2011, after the US market had closed, Steve Jobs resigned as chief executive of Apple in a letter to the board. He stayed on as chairman, and the chief operating officer, Tim Cook, replaced him, according to CNN and RTÉ. Apple's shares fell roughly 5% to $357 in after-hours trading, CNN reported; RTÉ said they had dropped as much as 7% at one point.

The initial fall did not set the longer course. On 2 August 2018, Apple's shares rose 2.8% to as high as $207.05, making it the world's first publicly listed company worth $1 trillion, Euronews reported. The succession had been an orderly one: the new chief executive was the company's long-standing number two, and the founder remained on the board when the change was announced.

Intel, 2024 to 2025: a surprise exit and a CFO in the interim seat

The closer parallel in structure is Intel. On Monday 2 December 2024, in what the Associated Press called a surprise announcement, Intel said Pat Gelsinger had retired as chief executive and stepped down from the board, effective 1 December, according to Investing.com. The chief financial officer, David Zinsner, and Michelle Johnston Holthaus, newly appointed chief executive of Intel Products, were named interim co-chief executives, and the independent chair, Frank Yeary, became interim executive chair. AFP later reported that the board had lost confidence in Gelsinger.

Intel's shares jumped in early trading that day but ended down 0.5%, the AP reported. Bloomberg noted that the shares fell 60% over 2024 as a whole. On Wednesday 12 March 2025, a little over three months after Gelsinger's exit, Intel named the chip industry veteran Lip-Bu Tan as its next chief executive, starting on 18 March, according to AFP. The shares jumped 15% the following day to $23.70, Bloomberg reported.

What is similar, and what is different

  • The interim structure resembles Intel's. At both Nvidia and Intel, the chief financial officer stepped into the top job on an interim basis while the board searched, and the outgoing chief executive also left the board.
  • The founder question resembles Apple's, but only in part. Apple and Nvidia were both led by a founder for many years. Jobs, however, stayed on as chairman and handed over to a successor named the same day; Huang has left the board, and no permanent successor has been named.
  • The business backdrop is different. Intel was making its change after a year in which its shares fell 60%. Nvidia's first results under interim leadership showed revenue more than doubling year on year, although its shares had fallen sharply in the month before the resignation.
  • The first move did not decide the outcome. Apple's after-hours fall in 2011 says little about what followed between then and 2018. Intel's biggest share move in this sequence came not at the exit but when a permanent leader was named.

What to watch

The useful questions now are factual ones. Whom does Nvidia's board choose as permanent chief executive, and is that person an insider or an outsider? How long does the search take, compared with the roughly three months Intel needed? And does the company deliver revenue in line with its guidance of about $130 billion for the current quarter?

Earlier leadership changes do not tell us how this one will go, and the two cases above came about in very different circumstances. They do suggest that the first day's share move is a poor guide to what follows, and that the choice of a permanent leader and the underlying business have mattered more.
