Brent Has Fallen Almost a Quarter in a Month. Here Is What Followed Earlier Oil War Spikes.

25 Nov 2026 4 min read Signal Intelligence Research

Oil has given back a war premium quickly before: in the 1990–91 Gulf War, US crude fell 58% from its October 1990 high, including a record one-day drop of about a third on 17 January 1991, and Brent ended 2022 at $85.91 after touching $139.13 in March. Brent was trading near $90.42 on 24 November, 23% below its level a month earlier, but shipping through the Strait of Hormuz is still being attacked, so the precedents describe past paths rather than a forecast.

On the morning of 24 November, Brent crude was trading at $90.42 a barrel, according to Fortune's daily price report. That was $1.63 below the previous morning, and 23.02% below where Brent stood a month earlier, at $117.47. Reuters, in a report carried by Kitco, said Brent had touched its lowest level since 27 August and US crude its weakest since 10 August, before steadying.

The fall has been quick. Gulf News reported that Brent closed above $117 on 30 October and had fallen roughly 24%, about $28 a barrel, from its late-October futures peak. In the week to 6 November alone, Dow Jones Newswires reported that oil was heading for a weekly loss of 14%, on hopes for a diplomatic solution between the US and Iran after the Democratic sweep of Congress. "Confidence has returned to the oil market," Commerzbank told Dow Jones.

The reasons given this week are mostly about supply. Reuters reported that Libya's Sharara pipeline had reopened, that China and India were on track for record Russian oil imports this year, and that Deutsche Bank saw global observed liquids inventories rising in October, implying an oversupply of 1.7 million barrels a day. Gulf News pointed to record Gulf flows helped by bypass routes and ship-to-ship transfers.

The risks have not gone away. Three vessels were struck by unidentified projectiles as they sailed through the Strait of Hormuz on 23 November, according to the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency, as reported by Reuters. Gulf News noted that the war began in late February, and Kotak Neo reported that Qatar is mediating talks in a conflict now nearing its ninth month. OPEC+ ministers meet on 29 November to decide output policy for 2027.

So readers are asking a fair question: when oil has spiked on a war before, how quickly did the premium fade, and what came next? Two modern episodes are the clearest guides.

1990 to 1991: the Gulf War and a record one-day fall

The first precedent is the 1990–91 Gulf War. Canada Energy Regulator data show that West Texas Intermediate rose 166%, from about $15 a barrel in June 1990 to about $41 in October 1990. From $41.07 on 11 October 1990, it then fell to $17.43 on 25 January 1991, a decline of 58% over 95 trading days.

The sharpest part came when the fighting began, not when it ended. US-led air strikes began on 16 January 1991, Oil & Gas Journal reported at the time. On 17 January, oil fell by $10.56, a record one-day drop of about 33%, according to CNN Money, as the US drew on its Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Brent, Oil & Gas Journal reported, closed on 17 January at $20.75, down from about $30 the day before. The journal cited early reports of successful air strikes with little Iraqi retaliation, and an International Energy Agency emergency plan to release 2.5 million barrels a day from stocks.

2022: Russia, Ukraine and a $139 peak

The second precedent is more recent. On 7 March 2022, after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, Brent hit $139.13 a barrel before falling back, Al Jazeera reported, the highest level since 2008.

The premium faded more slowly this time, and for different reasons. Reuters reported that prices "cooled rapidly in the second half" of 2022 as central banks raised interest rates and fanned worries of recession. Brent settled at $85.91 on the last trading day of the year, still up about 10% for 2022 after a 50% rise in 2021. In other words, prices ended the year well below the March peak but above where the year began.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Supply responses. In 1991, emergency stock releases helped turn the market. This year, UBS told Reuters that releases from strategic reserves are keeping supply relatively comfortable, so a similar tool is again part of the story.
  • The physical risk. In 1991 the sharpest fall came once the fighting appeared to be going one way. In 2026, shipping through Hormuz is still being attacked, and ANZ told Reuters that logistics remain constrained and inventory buffers are thin.
  • The scale of disruption. The International Energy Agency has described the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz this year as the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market, a far larger shock to flows than in 2022.
  • The rest of the economy. In 2022, rising interest rates did much of the work in the second half. This year the falls have been tied more closely to diplomacy and to supply returning, so the precedents are only a partial match.
  • Views differ. Even as prices fell, Kotak Neo reported that Goldman Sachs raised its December Brent forecast and warned of much higher prices in 2027 if Gulf disruption is prolonged. Forecasts from banks are opinions, not outcomes.

What to watch

The useful questions are factual. What do OPEC+ ministers decide on 29 November? Do US and Iran talks, with Qatar mediating, produce an agreement or stall? Do attacks on shipping near Hormuz continue, and do Gulf flows hold up? And does the inventory build that Deutsche Bank described continue into November and December?

The precedents do not tell us how this episode will end. They show that oil has given back a war premium before, sometimes in a single day, as in January 1991, and sometimes over months, as in 2022, and that the reasons were different each time. Conditions now are different again. Signal Intelligence will keep tracking the facts as they come in.
