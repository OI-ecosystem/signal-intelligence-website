Pre-Owned Watch Prices Are Rising Again. Here Is How the Last Two Watch Slumps Ended.

13 Nov 2026 4 min read Signal Intelligence Research

The watch industry has climbed out of two slumps since 2008: Swiss watch exports rebounded 22.1% in 2010 after falling 22.3% in 2009, but rose only 2.7% in 2017 after two years of decline. Pre-owned prices have now risen for five straight quarters yet remain about 29% below their 2022 peak, and record inventory is what sets this recovery apart.

Pre-owned luxury watch prices rose 1.2% in the third quarter of 2026 on the WatchCharts market index, and were up 8.3% on a year earlier, WatchPro reported on 20 October. The Morgan Stanley report built on that data found a fifth consecutive quarter of price increases above 1%. Rolex was the laggard: on WatchCharts' measure its prices rose 0.5% in the quarter, the smallest quarterly gain the brand has posted in seven quarters.

The recovery starts from a low base. The overall WatchCharts index is still about 29% below its April 2022 peak, after 11 straight quarters of declines that followed it, according to reporting on the Morgan Stanley figures. And supply has been growing faster than prices. Morgan Stanley found that secondary-market inventory across the six biggest brands had increased by almost 20% year on year, which WatchPro noted could limit further price rises.

New watches tell a mixed story too. Swiss watch exports grew for a fifth straight month in September, Bloomberg reported, and rose 2.7% to 19.5 billion francs in the first nine months of the year, according to the Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry. Exports to the US jumped 86% in September, partly because of a tariff-related slump a year earlier, while exports to China declined.

So the question many collectors and dealers are asking is a fair one: has the watch market climbed out of a slump like this before, and what did the recovery look like? It has, twice since 2008, and the two recoveries were very different.

The 2022 resale bubble, for scale

The pre-owned market boomed into the first half of 2022. At the peak, The Business of Fashion reported, some sought-after models were trading at three or four times their retail prices. By the autumn of 2022 it reported that the overall market index had fallen by more than a quarter from its peak. By October 2023, Axios reported that the WatchCharts Overall Market Index had fallen more than 35% since March 2022. A gap of about 29% today means part of that fall has been regained, not all of it.

2009: a 22% fall, then a fast rebound

The first precedent is the financial crisis. Swiss watch exports fell 22.3% in 2009 to 13.2 billion francs, 3.8 billion francs less than in 2008, according to the Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry. Monthly exports held at between 20% and 33% below the previous year until October, before the declines narrowed to single digits in November and December.

The rebound came quickly. In 2010 exports rose 22.1% to 16.2 billion francs, which the federation described as a return to a healthy and robust situation, while noting that the sector had still to return to its 2008 level. In 2011 exports reached a record 19.3 billion francs, up 19.2%, AFP reported, despite the strong franc.

2015 and 2016: a slower slump, and a modest recovery

The second precedent unfolded more slowly. Swiss watch exports fell for the second year in succession in 2016, by 9.9% to 19.4 billion francs, which Bloomberg called the worst performance since 2009. The federation said the industry had returned to its 2011 level, ending the growth of 15% achieved between 2011 and 2014. Exports to Hong Kong, then the industry's largest market, fell 25.1%. The federation pointed to weak demand for personal luxury goods, the strong franc, lower European tourism and measures taken by the Chinese government.

The recovery that followed was modest. In 2017 exports rose 2.7% to 19.9 billion francs. China, up 18.8%, was the first market to recover, and Hong Kong ended the year up 6.0%.

What is similar, and what is different

  • Different measures. Export figures count new watches shipped from Switzerland; the WatchCharts index tracks prices for pre-owned watches. The precedents describe the industry as a whole, not resale prices, so they show the shape of past recoveries rather than offering a like-for-like comparison.
  • Speed. After 2009 the rebound came within a year and a record within two. After 2016 the recovery was slower and smaller. The pre-owned index has so far risen for five quarters, after 11 quarters of declines.
  • Supply. The distinctive feature of 2026 is inventory. Morgan Stanley's figure of almost 20% more secondary-market inventory across the six biggest brands, and what Forbes called a 19% jump in listings, point to more watches for sale even as prices rise.
  • Geography. Both earlier recoveries leaned heavily on Asia, and China and Hong Kong led the 2017 upturn. This September, exports to China declined while the US grew strongly, partly from a low, tariff-hit base.

What to watch

The useful questions are factual ones. Does the quarterly rise in pre-owned prices continue when the next WatchCharts and Morgan Stanley figures are published? Does inventory keep growing faster than prices? And do Swiss export figures for the last months of the year keep growing while shipments to China fall?

The precedents do not tell us how this cycle will end. They show that the watch industry has recovered from slumps before, once quickly and once slowly, and that the pace depended on demand in a few key markets and on conditions that could not be seen at the low.
